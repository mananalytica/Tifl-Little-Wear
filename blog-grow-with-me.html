Grow-With-Me

Kids clothes that actually grow with them — how our expandable seams work

Published 18 August 2026 · 6 min read · Tifl Little Wear, Lahore

"Grow with me clothes" gets searched by Lahore parents a lot more than you'd think — usually right after buying something their child outgrew in four months. It's a fair complaint. Kids don't grow on a schedule that matches retail seasons, and most rack-bought clothing is cut for exactly one size, for exactly one moment. We build something different into almost every piece that leaves our Gulberg studio: growing room, stitched in by hand, from the very first pattern draft.

We call it ek silaai, teen saal — one stitch, three years — and it's less a slogan than a literal description of the technique.

What "growing room" actually means

It isn't a bigger size. A garment cut two sizes up just hangs wrong today so it might fit later — loose shoulders, drooping hems, sleeves a child has to keep pushing back. Growing room is different: the garment fits properly now, with deliberate, hidden allowance built into specific seams — usually the side seams, waist, and hem — that can be let out later without re-cutting the piece or leaving visible stitch marks.

Where the extra fabric hides

When our tailor drafts a pattern from a child's 11-point measurement card, a few extra centimetres get folded into the seam allowance at key growth points rather than trimmed away. It's invisible from the outside — the garment looks and fits like any well-tailored piece. Inside, there's a deliberate fold of fabric waiting at the seam, ready to be released by hand when it's needed.

Letting a piece out — what that visit actually looks like

When a piece starts feeling snug, it doesn't mean the end of its life — it usually means a short visit back to the studio. Our tailor opens the hidden allowance at the relevant seam, re-presses it, and the garment moves up with the child. It's a fraction of the time and cost of a new outfit, and for occasion pieces especially, it means a favourite kurta or frock doesn't have to be retired after a single Eid.

Why this matters more than a size chart

Standard size charts assume every 4-year-old measures the same. They don't — and growth doesn't happen evenly either; a child can shoot up in height while staying the same everywhere else, which is exactly the kind of change a rack size can't account for but a hand-let seam can. It's also, quietly, the more sustainable choice: fewer outfits worn twice and outgrown, more pieces that earn their keep over a couple of years.

Which pieces this works best for

Everyday wear and occasion pieces both get growing room by default. It's most noticeable on structured pieces — kurtas, frocks, waistcoats — where a size jump is usually the most visible. If you're planning ahead for a specific event, our tailor can also build in extra allowance deliberately, so a piece bought this Eid still has a season or two left in it for the next one.
