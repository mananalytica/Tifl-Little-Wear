The people behind the stitching

The one tailor behind every occasion piece

Published 23 August 2026 · 5 min read · Tifl Little Wear, Lahore

Ustad Abdul Sattar · Master Tailor · 24 years

Ask any parent who orders occasion wear from Tifl who actually made the piece, and there's a good chance the answer is one name: Ustad Abdul Sattar. That's not a coincidence and not a marketing line — every occasion piece in our in-house line is drafted, cut, and finished by his hands. So we thought it was time to introduce him properly.

Where the training came from

Ustad Sattar didn't learn tailoring at a fashion school. In the year 2000, at fourteen, he sat across from his father in a small tailoring shop in Multan and began the apprenticeship that would define how he works today. That's what "a Multan training lineage" means to the studio: patterns checked against the eye, embroidery marked without templates, seams finished the way his father's shop did them for decades.

By 2008 he'd opened his own little occasion-wear shop, specialising in hand embroidery for weddings. And in 2010 he moved to Lahore to lead the make-to-measure and occasion-wear line for Tifl Little Wear. Sixteen years later, he's still here — and in that time the studio has watched him cut more than 3,000 garments.

No size charts, ever

The first thing people notice about his work is also the hardest to photograph: every single pattern is hand-drafted from the child's own measurements. We don't keep a rack of sizes in the studio that goods get cleared to. A chest measurement of 57cm and one of 58cm get genuinely different patterns, chalked on fabric and cut by hand. For occasion wear, where a wedding lehenga or sherwani only gets one wear that counts, that draft is the whole ball game.

The embroidery that's his signature

Ask him what he enjoys most and it's the hand-set embroidery on festive and bridal-side pieces. The pieces marked "by Ustad Sattar" — the lehngas, the sherwanis, the heavyweight waistcoat sets — carry embroidery that's placed by eye, mark by mark, rather than copied off a reference. When the gulabi anarkali went out of the studio for a twelve-year-old this season, the dupatta work came together in a single sitting, and the fit came back on the first check.

The portfolio, in plain terms

A few of the pieces he's cut and finished recently, all drafted from fresh measurements:

  • Silk waistcoat set — hand-cut, for weddings and formal occasions
  • Embroidered mini lehna with dupatta — the hand-embroidery above
  • Cotton gharara set with delicate hand embroidery
  • Quilted winter sherwani — hand-lined and finished for cooler months

Now he's teaching the next set

Somewhere in the last few years, his role shifted. He still cuts every occasion piece personally, but he also trains the studio's newer tailors — twelve apprentices and counting. Ask the younger stitches who taught them to let a seam settle before trimming it, and they'll point to him. When you book a fitting "with the master tailor," that's who you get: the same hands, on your own child's measurements, with twenty-four years of knowing where a five-year-old is going to want room in a sleeve.

If you'd like a private fitting with him directly — for a wedding piece, embroidery work, or anything that needs his specific eye — you can book with him here, see all the studio's master tailors, or start with a standard studio fitting.
